LT PDGA Statistics

General Information

This application gathers hole-by-hole scores and calculates and displays statistics for Lithuanian PDGA tournaments.

Lithuanian PDGA tournaments used hole-by-hole scores from PDGA Live sporadically from late 2021 through 2022. Since 2023, every tournament has included them, so the statistics cover this period.

At the moment, unavailable competitions have been processed, and unavailable players have submitted scores. The latest included competition is unavailable.

Hole Layouts

The application combines results for each hole across multiple tournaments. A hole might have multiple layouts when it changes significantly; these layouts are tracked separately.

It is a judgment call whether a change to a hole necessitates a new layout (i.e., whether it significantly affects scoring).

For example, the introduction of new OB lines on holes 1, 2, and 3 in Pušynas affected hole 2 much more than holes 1 and 3, so a new layout was created for hole 2 but not for holes 1 and 3. A change to the hazard rule was very important for hole 4 in Domeikava, but the same change was much less noticeable for hole 3 in Pučkoriai. Similar logic was applied to multiple tee-pad changes in Domeikava by checking whether the length or angle of a shot changed enough.

Ratings

All statistics are based on PDGA ratings. Ratings are taken as inputs (a rating with which a player enters a round) and are emulated in various calculations.

There is no published description of exactly how PDGA round ratings are calculated, but essentially, the process is a form of linear regression that creates a line corresponding to results and ratings. In this application, the same process is emulated using a standard "least squares" approach. It might not match the PDGA logic exactly, but it should be conceptually close.

The main differences are that ratings are calculated for each hole separately and aggregated across multiple rounds and tournaments. The latter has consequences when combining rounds with different conditions, but it allows us to collect, combine, and process many more results.

SPR/VAR Calculations

Applying an emulated rating calculation produces a line corresponding to score and rating for each hole layout.

SPR is a measure of hole difficulty: it indicates how much scoring separation higher-rated players can create on a hole. It is the slope of a line (more precisely, the slope multiplied by -100 to make the numbers positive and easier to read). This represents the expected score difference between players with a 100-point rating difference. For instance, if the SPR value is 1 and the expected score on a hole for a player rated 900 is 4, the expected score for a player rated 850 should be 4.5, and for a player rated 800, 5, and so on.

VAR is a measure of hole variability: it indicates how diverse the scores on a hole are. It is the average difference between expected scores and actual scores.

There is a strong correlation between hole length and SPR value, which is clearly visible on the chart, with longer holes generally appearing on the right side of the chart. However, other factors also play a role: holes with challenging lines and difficult OBs tend to have higher SPR values. In a certain sense, SPR can be thought of as a measure of a hole's difficulty.

Variability might also depend on the factors mentioned above, as length and difficulty affect scoring diversity. However, the correlation between SPR and VAR is not always strong, as there are multiple examples of high VAR on relatively low-SPR holes.

SPR is the expected score difference between players with a 100-point rating difference, but a natural question is whether the score difference stays the same across all ratings (i.e., whether the expected score difference between a player rated 1000 and one rated 900 is the same as that between a player rated 900 and one rated 800). Theoretically, the answer is yes: because the rating is calculated as a linear function, the expected score difference is also linear. In practice, however, no hole can produce uniform scores across all ratings. The next section analyzes how a hole's layout affects its scoring across different ratings.

SPRW Calculations

SPR calculates scoring separation based on all results. It shows an average scoring separation but potentially hides more granular scoring details. SPRW is an SPR calculation using weighted results to highlight a specific rating region. For a rating N, it is calculated using ratings from the [N-50, N+50] rating window, with a "triangular weight" applied to the scores. For instance, SPRW calculated for a rating of 900 includes all scores from players rated 900 with a weight of 1, scores from players rated 890 or 910 with a weight of 0.8, scores from players rated 865 or 935 with a weight of 0.3, and so on.

The SPRW chart displays SPRW across the range of ratings with enough recorded scores. The total weight in the range should be at least 50. SPRW values with a total weight from 50 to 100 are displayed as unconnected dots, indicating lower confidence; values from 100 to 200 are displayed as dotted lines; and values above 200 are displayed as solid lines.

A straightforward interpretation of SPRW is that it provides a more precise way of determining the expected scoring difference. To understand scoring between players rated 890 and 910, SPRW at a rating of 900 is much more useful than the general SPR.

There is also another way of looking at it: by considering a single player. A player of a given rating would have performance on a course, a hole, or any particular shot centered around that rating, most likely with performance following some form of normal distribution. A high SPRW for this player means that shot quality matters more and is more likely to affect the score, resulting in a more challenging experience. A low SPRW, on the other hand, might mean that shot quality does not matter as much, either because the score is "funneled" toward a particular result or because the hole is too random for shot quality to correlate with the overall result.

In that sense, peaks in the SPRW chart might be interpreted as an indication of a hole's "target audience": the rating range the hole suits best. It is important to keep absolute values in mind, however. On long, difficult holes, even the minimum SPRW values might be higher than those of shorter and simpler holes.

Scoring Distribution

Scoring distribution shows the percentages of occurrences for specific scores, with scores of 1 and 2 grouped together, as well as all scores above 8. On the “Course stats” tab, these percentages are provided for the full range of results or for players within a specific rating range when a filter is selected.

The chart on the “Hole stats” tab takes a slightly different approach: it calculates scoring around specific ratings using the same “triangular weight” method used by SPRW. For instance, 50% for score 4 at a rating of 850 does not mean that half of the players with a rating of exactly 850 played the hole with a score of 4, since there were likely not many players with that exact rating. Instead, it means that a score of 4 was recorded approximately half the time among all players around the 850 rating.

The lines on the chart correspond to specific scores or groups of scores. A line is available if the score has ever occurred on the hole. Each line can be toggled on or off. By default, to avoid clutter, only lines representing scores that occurred at least 5% of the time overall are enabled.

Personal Stats. Hole Stats

This section shows a player's performance rating for each hole. Using the linear regression described in the previous sections, each score on any hole can be assigned a projected rating, and the average rating across all recorded results is displayed. These values are approximate measurements of a player's performance on every hole, and they provide a general idea of how well each hole has historically been played.

Note that enough plays might be needed to stabilize the results and produce reasonable values. With a low number of plays, some extreme rating values might be produced.

Personal stats. Putting stats

Putting statistics are calculated from statistics entered by players in the PDGA Live app. For a round to be included, statistics must be fully entered for all holes. Incomplete rounds are shown in the round details with strikethrough formatting, but they do not affect overall or yearly values.

The statistics include:

  1. C1x. Percentage of made putts from Circle 1 excluding the bullseye (3–10 meters).
  2. C2. Percentage of made putts from Circle 2 (10–20 meters).
  3. C2+ per round. Average number of putts and throw-ins from C2 and beyond (10+ meters) per round. This value is weighted for rounds with more than 18 holes.
  4. Most C2+ in a round. Maximum number of putts and throw-ins from C2+ in a single round, calculated for the selected year or overall.
  5. Best C1x round. Highest C1x percentage achieved in a single round, calculated for the selected year or overall. The total number of putts in the round is used as a secondary criterion.
  6. Longest throw-in. Longest recorded distance from which a hole was completed. The distance must be entered in the statistics to be counted. Aces are included when the distance is entered.
  7. Lost 3+putting per round. Average number of strokes lost to 3+ putting per round, including 3-putts, 4-putts, 5-putts, and so on. This value is weighted for rounds with more than 18 holes. A 3+ putt occurs when a player fails to complete a hole within one or two throws after first landing in C1 or C2. Penalty strokes are included. Every throw beyond the second counts as a stroke lost to 3+ putting.

Leaderboards

All leaderboards except Aces display the top 10 players for various putting metrics. Circle 2 percentage and 3+putting are not included because the back edge of Circle 2 can be difficult to define consistently.

Each player can appear only once on a leaderboard. For example, a player's result on the Longest throw-in leaderboard is their best result. When a player achieves the same best result multiple times, the number of occurrences is used as a tie-breaker. This applies to Most C2+ in a round, Best C1x round, and Longest throw-in.

The aggregated C1x and C2+ per round leaderboards require a minimum number of putts thrown or holes played. The thresholds are shown below the relevant leaderboard.

The Aces leaderboard is different: it does not use player-entered statistics, but draws directly from results and hole information. Therefore, it can include data from earlier years and does not require players to enter statistics. It effectively captures all aces recorded in PDGA tournaments with live scoring. A player may appear multiple times on this leaderboard. To avoid duplication aces are excluded from the "Longest throw-in" leaderboard.
